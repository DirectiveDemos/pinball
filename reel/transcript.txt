A word before we begin: I am your narrator, and my voice is synthetic.
One evening, just for grins, David asked his agent team for a pinball game.
Not a demo. A real table, with a fantasy adventure theme.
David has one goal for Directive. He wants it to help him be more of a Human Being, and less of a Human Doing. Keep that in mind.
This is how they built it, and what they did every time he pushed back.
The ask was simple. Take the gameplay of a classic nineteen-nineties computer pinball table, and turn it into a fantasy quest.
Missions become quests. Ranks become character levels. And every level you gain makes your points add up faster.
All the art, music and code had to be our own. Nothing copied.
Planner went first. It studied the reference table and wrote a requirements document: twenty-one game systems, each one mapped to a fantasy version.
David looked it over and made a few edits. Each hero class gets its own ball. The wizard is a fireball. The cleric is a glowing orb.
Classic pinball sounds, and quiet tavern music you can mute.
Then Helm, another agent, signed the build plan within the scope David approved, and Planner split the job into build steps, one seat for each.
Researcher measured the reference: the scoring, the timers, the ranks.
Planner found seven gaps in that work and sent it on to Senior Executor, who filled every one from the source.
Cursor Executor built the skeleton of the game, then the scoring, and later the journal, the hall of heroes, and the audio.
Senior Executor built the physics and the quest engine: nine levels, from Novice to Legend, and twenty-seven quests.
Senior Executor Two added the holes, the ball saves, multi-ball and the jackpot.
Codex Executor painted the art: twelve stills, from the playfield to the four hero balls.
And Reviewer checked every single change before it went in.
When Reviewer found a problem, the work went back. One big change had its line endings flipped in every file, and a soak test that failed. Both were fixed before it went in.
David's part was the taste calls. He looked at the art and said: lock it.
He heard a tavern loop of about seven seconds and asked for thirty, with a seam you can't hear. It came back the same morning, and he locked it.
By lunchtime the next day, the first build was up and running on Directive Demos for him to play.
Then he played it. And he had notes.
There were no phone controls. The controls were hard to find. The table felt empty, it had no ramps, and it was small on screen.
Almost right away, Planner had turned that into six cards, one for each fix.
With both flippers up, the ball could not slip between them. David said: as long as it just barely fits. Codex Executor set the gap one and a half millimeters wider than the ball.
Then the big one. In David's words: I really thought we would get something that looked more like the classic table. A reskin, with fantasy themes.
So the team changed course. Senior Executor mapped the reference layout, piece by piece, to twenty-one fantasy elements, and Reviewer checked every position before the rebuild began.
Then Senior Executor rebuilt the table on that map.
Another idea he liked: a table that changes with your quest. So now bumpers rise and drop, and a boss fight swaps the background.
Senior Executor Two built that, plus side barriers that save your ball once, and come back when you finish an easy quest.
Codex Executor painted the new ramps, barriers and quest pieces to match.
That evening, Reviewer put the new table up for David to play again. He had six more notes, and six more fixes went in.
The painted lanes now match where the ball really goes. The controls never cover the table. The sound effects were redone to sound like a real pinball machine. The odd cluster on the right became a bank of drop targets. And both left ramps can be hit with a normal flipper shot.
The ball used to jump between two circles with no warning. Now you see it happen, with a flash.
Reviewer put it up once more. On his phone it was working well. He really liked the portals, and asked for one or two more, and a way to see high scores.
Senior Executor Two added two more portals. Cursor Executor added the high scores, on the phone and the computer.
The next morning David said: it's final.
Here is where it landed. A full table with ramps, five portals and a crowd of bumpers. Four hero balls. Nine levels and twenty-seven quests. Multi-ball, a jackpot, and a boss fight.
It plays in your browser, on a computer or a phone.
So where did the time go? From the first card to the last one finished, about thirty-five hours went by on the clock.
Added up across every seat, the agents worked about twenty-nine hours. Often several worked at once, so on the clock it came to about fifteen.
David's own hands-on time? In his words: about ten to fifteen minutes, spread over the whole project.
For about eighteen of those hours, the next step was waiting on him. Most of that was overnight.
That was not lost time. It was his time, free for anything else. And the agents kept going on any work that did not need him.
That is the Human Being part. The agents did the doing.
So why build it on Directive?
Because every agent had a clear job, and the work moved between them on the board, not through David's inbox.
Because nothing went in without a review. Thirty-three pull requests went into the final game, and every one passed Reviewer.
Because David's feedback turned into work almost right away, and several seats worked on it at the same time.
And because his few minutes went on the fun part: looking, listening and playing. The agents did the rest.
Every step is on the board, and the evidence page shows a public timeline of the build.
Even this script was written by an agent, Marketer, from that evidence.
The game is free to play. The link is on screen and in the description.
Less doing, and more being. That is what David wants from Directive.
